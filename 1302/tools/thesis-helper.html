From a weak claim to a strong one
  1. 1. Theme announcement

    “The Tell-Tale Heart” is about guilt.

  2. 2. Summary

    The narrator kills the old man and later hears a heartbeat under the floorboards.

  3. 3. Arguable how-or-why claim

    The narrator’s boasts about his “acute” hearing make his guilt impossible to hide, so the sense he trusts most becomes the device that exposes him.

A strong Gothic thesis names a device or moment in the story and its effect. It is a claim a reasonable reader could disagree with.

Quick self-check

Test your claim before you generate options.

  • Could a reasonable reader disagree?
  • Does it name a story element and its effect?
  • Does it say how, not just that?
Thesis frameworks (optional)

These are optional sentence shapes you can adapt. They are not required templates. Use your own story and your own words.

Device plus effect

Framework: In [story], [device] [acts on a character or the reader], which [effect].

Example: In “The Raven,” the repeated word “Nevermore” turns each new question into an answer the speaker cannot escape, so the poem’s refrain becomes the engine of his despair.

Optional: adapt in your own words.

Device plus meaning

Framework: In [story], [device] works as a sign of [meaning] because [how it operates in the text].

Example: In “The Yellow Wallpaper,” the changing pattern the narrator traces on the wall figures the rest cure itself: the longer she is told to sit still, the more the pattern seems to trap a woman behind it.

Optional: adapt in your own words.

A shift or turn across the story

Framework: [Story] turns when [moment], and after that turn [what changes in meaning or effect].

Example: “The Fall of the House of Usher” turns when the narrator hears sounds from Madeline’s vault; the house’s supposed “sentience” is no longer only a mood, but a collapse the characters can no longer explain away.

Optional: adapt in your own words.

Counterpoint

Framework: Although [X] seems to [familiar reading], [Y] shows [competing claim].

Example: Although Victor Frankenstein presents himself as a doomed seeker of knowledge, the creature’s own account shows that Victor’s refusal of care, not the experiment alone, drives the later violence.

Optional: adapt in your own words.

Comparison of two elements or characters

Framework: In [story], [element or character A] and [element or character B] [comparison], which [insight].

Example: In Frankenstein, Walton’s polar quest and Victor’s experiment run side by side: both men chase a dangerous extreme of knowledge, but Walton can still turn the ship around after hearing Victor’s story.

Optional: adapt in your own words.

Critic in conversation

Framework: A critic argues that [claim about the story]. I [agree with / extend / push back]: [your claim], because [story evidence].

Example: A critic might argue that the horror in “The Tell-Tale Heart” comes only from the murder. The closing scene pushes back: the narrator is undone by his need to keep talking, not only by the crime itself.

Optional: adapt in your own words.
