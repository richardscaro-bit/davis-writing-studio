Case Study research guide

Purpose

This page is a research map for the Case Study Critical Analysis. Use it when you are ready to find, judge, and log the exactly 2 outside sources that go with your packet.

Use this page to

  • See what kinds of sources this essay needs
  • Follow a search plan in Google Scholar and the SAC Library
  • Judge a result, then log and cite your two sources

The rule for this essay

Your question and thesis

  • Start with your own critical analysis question about your case study.
  • You can find example critical questions in your Case Brief file.
  • Your thesis answers your question and previews all five lenses, in order.

Five body sections

  • In order, they are Framing, Stakeholders, Evidence, Assumptions, and Limits.
  • Your outline has five body sections, one for each lens, in that order.
  • Put 2 cited case study details in each section.
  • Put your limitations or ethics idea in the Limits section.
  • Do not add a separate limitations section outside of Limits.

Your sources

  • Your case study packet is your primary source. Use it in every body section.
  • Use exactly 2 outside sources from Google Scholar or the SAC Library.
  • Every body section uses your packet plus one or both outside sources.
  • You can use either outside source in any section, and as often as you need.

Length: 1,000 to 1,500 words, MLA (AI Level 2: support, not composition). Turn in: the Outline / Research Plan assignment in Canvas.

What to look for

What kinds of sources this essay needs

Your case file

  • Your Source Packet and Case Brief are the primary readings.

Outside sources

  • Outside sources have a narrower job.
  • They check a packet detail, then help you analyze how strong that detail is.
  • Start with the packet. Choose one topic and stay with it. Read the Case Options, the matching Case Brief, and the matching Source Packet before you search.
  • Then find exactly 2 outside sources. Every body section uses your packet plus one or both outside sources. You can use either outside source in any section, and as often as you need.
  • Pick details that need a check. Good candidates are a number, a broad success story, or a statement that comes only from the company or group itself.
  • Prefer sources you can cite. Scholarly articles, studies, government or agency reports, and substantial news investigations usually work better than a random blog post or an unsourced chart.
  • A source that disagrees is useful. If a study or report cuts against what the packet says, it can help you judge where the packet is strong or thin.
  • AI output is not evidence. The tutor and other AI tools may help you think, but they do not count as outside sources. Do not cite an AI summary of an article you have not read.

Give each source a purpose line

  • Every outside source needs a purpose line you can finish in one sentence: Use this to ___, not to ___.

If you cannot finish that line

  • The source may be interesting, but it is not ready for a body section.

Step by step

A search plan

  1. Name the packet detail you want to check. Write: The packet says ___ (page or slide ___). I want to check whether ___. Do this for the two details that most need outside proof.
  2. Look in your topic folder first. If that folder has a Research subfolder, start there and use a source from it when it fits.
  3. Turn the detail into search words. Pull out the case or company name, the topic, and the idea you want to test. Add a support word (study, data, report) or a contradict word (criticism, cancel, risk, lawsuit). The keyword generator can help you build strings.
  4. Search SAC Library databases. Sign in with ACES when prompted. Start with the databases below, then use the A to Z list if you need a subject database.
  5. Use Google Scholar to discover, then come back for the full text. If Scholar shows only an abstract, search the title in SAC databases or ask a librarian.
  6. Sort each keeper. Does it support the packet detail, contradict it, or add context? Then judge who is behind it before you trust it.
  7. Log the two keepers. Put each one in the Source Log with a purpose line, a credibility note, and an MLA citation. Then use each source in the body sections where it helps, after the packet details and before your analysis. Every body section uses your packet plus one or both outside sources.

Library search

Recommended SAC databases

Where to search

  • Prefer SAC Library databases for your outside sources.
  • Off campus, log in with ACES.
  • If a paywall appears, ask a librarian. Campus access often unlocks the article.

How these links work

  • Each database name below opens that title on the official SAC A to Z list.
  • Click the title, then sign in if you are asked.
  • These five are a strong start for Case Study research. They appear on the SAC list as of this guide.
  • Academic OneFile. A broad database of journals, magazines, and news. Use it when you want studies or reporting that test a packet detail.
  • JSTOR. Scholarly journal articles, usually as PDFs. Coverage often stops a few years before the present, so pair it with a current-events database if you need recent data.
  • Opposing Viewpoints In Context. Background, viewpoint essays, and related news on contested issues. Useful when you need context for a public debate around your case.
  • CQ Researcher. In-depth reports on current political and social issues, with background and more than one side. Good when your packet sits inside a larger policy or social question.
  • Nexis Uni (listed as Nexis Uni | LexisNexis Academic). News, business, and company information. Useful for reporting, complaints, or coverage that may support or contradict a success story.

Need a subject database instead (education, health, psychology, and so on)? Browse the full A to Z list, or start from SAC Academic Guides.

Discovery

Google Scholar tips

Google Scholar is useful for finding scholarly articles and seeing who cited them. Pair it with SAC access so you can get the full text.

  • Open Scholar, then go to Settings, Library links. Search for Alamo or San Antonio College so Full-Text links appear.
  • Put quotation marks around an exact phrase: "subscription fatigue".
  • Combine ideas with AND and OR: "subscription box" AND loyalty AND (study OR data).
  • Use the date range in the left sidebar when your topic needs recent evidence.
  • Under a good result, use Cited by or Related articles to find more.
  • Prefer library PDFs over random upload sites. If Scholar shows only an abstract, search the title in the SAC A to Z list or ask a librarian.

Search words

Keywords and search strings

Build a short word list first

  • Include the case or company name, the topic, and the idea you want to test.

Then add a direction word

  • Add a support word or a contradict word.
  • To look for support, try words such as study, data, survey, results, effect, report, growth.
  • To look for a contradiction, try words such as criticism, problems, lawsuit, complaint, decline, risks, limits, cancel, fails.
  • Use quotation marks for an exact phrase. Use OR between words that mean the same thing. Use AND between different ideas.

Fill-in patterns you can paste into a database or Scholar:

  • [company or topic] AND [criterion word] AND (study OR data)
  • [company or topic] AND [idea] AND (criticism OR lawsuit OR report)
  • "[exact phrase]" AND [topic]
A practice pattern (not a source to cite)

This example uses the BarkBox practice case so you can see the shape of a search. It is a pattern, not a real article. Do not cite it as evidence.

Packet detail to check (Evidence): the materials present social content and community identity as drivers of subscription success.

  • Support search: "subscription box" AND "brand community" AND (loyalty OR study)
  • Contradict search: ("pet subscription" OR BarkBox) AND (churn OR cancel) AND (data OR report)

If you later find a real study or report, log that source. Do not invent a finding to match the pattern.

Source evaluation

Judge a source before you use it

Do not stay only on the result

  • Leave the page and read laterally.
  • Open a new tab and see what other sources say about the author, the publisher, or the finding.

A short method

  • Use SIFT, from Mike Caulfield. The four moves are below.
  • Stop. Pause before you treat a result as evidence. Notice your first impression, then check the source.
  • Investigate the source. Who is behind it: a researcher, a news outlet, a government agency, or the company itself? Search the author or publisher in a new tab.
  • Find better coverage. What do other reliable sources say about the same idea? You are checking the finding, not only this one page.
  • Trace it back. If the page quotes a study, a number, or another article, follow that trail to the original and see what it actually said.

Then hold the source next to your packet detail and sort it:

  • Supports if it shows the same thing the packet says.
  • Contradicts if it shows something different or the opposite.
  • Adds context if it explains background but does not prove or disprove the packet detail.

Fill in: This source ___ (supports, contradicts, adds context) the packet detail that ___. Also note: ___ is a ___ (researcher, news outlet, agency, company). Other sources ___. It was published in ___.

A company page can still be useful, but treat it as an interested source. Ask whether an independent study, report, or investigation says the same thing.

Read Caulfield's short SIFT overview if you want the original explanation of the four moves.

Keep what you found

Take notes in the Source Log

What to record

  • Log a source while you still have it open.
  • For each of your 2 outside sources, record the title, author, date, where you found it, and the link.
  • Also record which body sections it will serve, which packet detail it checks, and how it relates (supports, contradicts, or adds context).
  • Add a credibility note, a purpose line, and a draft MLA citation.

Where to log it

  • You can work in the Studio Source Log on this site (it saves in your browser on this device).
  • Or make your own copy of the course Source Log document.

Citation

MLA help

Works Cited

  • Your Works Cited should include the packet and both outside sources.

In-text citations

  • Every body section cites two packet details in the text.
  • Every body section also cites one or both outside sources in the text.
  • Many databases and Google Scholar have a Cite button. Choose MLA, copy the entry into your Source Log, then check it. Cite buttons make mistakes.
  • Give each outside source a signal phrase and an MLA in-text citation.
  • In every body section, use this order: packet detail, then one or both outside sources, then your analysis. Make your analysis at least as long as your evidence.

A fill-in pattern for that order:

  • Packet detail: The packet states that ___ (Packet Author page).
  • Outside source: According to [author or group], ___ (Author page).
  • Analysis: This shows that the packet ___ because ___.
