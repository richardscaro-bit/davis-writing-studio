When to quote, paraphrase, or summarize

Quote

Use a quote when the exact wording matters: a phrase you will analyze, a distinctive word, or language that would lose force if you rewrote it.

Paraphrase

Paraphrase when the idea matters more than the exact wording, and you can say that idea accurately in your own sentence shape.

Summarize

Summarize when you need the main point of a longer passage or a whole source, in a compact statement of your own.

All three still need a citation. A paraphrase or summary without a citation looks like the idea is yours.

How to do each in MLA

Quote

Introduce the quote, put the exact words in quotation marks, then add an in-text citation.

A library or Google Scholar source notes the same pattern: "Signup is offered only in English" (Elmhurst). That limit matters because it leaves out households the program claims to serve.

Paraphrase

Read, cover the source, write the idea in your own words, then check that you did not keep the source's sentence shape. Cite it anyway.

The invented newsletter says the compost pickup will cut landfill waste in half, but signup is only in English and pickup is limited to single-family homes (Elmhurst).

Summarize

State the source's main point in one or two sentences of your own, then cite it.

The invented newsletter promises a large drop in landfill waste, then describes a signup and pickup plan that covers only some households (Elmhurst).

The quote sandwich

Four parts

  1. A signal phrase that names the source or sets up the point
  2. The quote, in quotation marks
  3. An MLA in-text citation
  4. An explanation in your own words: why the quote matters for your claim

Do not drop a quote in alone

A quote cannot do the work by itself. Your sentence before it tells readers why it is there. Your sentence after it says what you want them to notice.

When a body paragraph uses a library or Google Scholar source, introduce it, cite it, and explain how it helps your claim.

Avoiding patchwriting

What it looks like

Patchwriting keeps the source's sentence shape and swaps a few words. It is a common stage in learning academic language, not a reason to panic. It is not yet a paraphrase, and it is not yet in your voice.

Too close: Households that join the weekly compost pickup will cut waste in half by year end, and signup is offered only in English (Elmhurst).

A better version

Cover the source. Write the idea as you would explain it to a classmate. Then uncover the source and check. If the sentences still match, rewrite again and cite.

In your own shape: The program claims a sharp drop in landfill waste, yet it is advertised only in English and only to single-family homes (Elmhurst).

Short practice

Read the whole passage first. Hide it before you write a gist or a paraphrase. Then show it again and check for close copying. Your work stays in this browser.

Practice passage

The passage is hidden. Write from memory, then show it to check your work.

Use (Elmhurst) as a practice citation for this invented passage. In a real essay, use the author and page or location from your source.
