Step 0: Why and how to add library or Google Scholar sources

Teachers sometimes call these outside sources.

Why

  1. Your packet shows what the company or group says about itself. A scholarly source lets you test that.
  2. Scholars give you expert terms and real data, so your analysis is not just your opinion.
  3. A source shows that your thesis is part of a bigger conversation that other people care about.
  4. Readers trust a writer who checks the story from more than one side.

How to add a source to a paragraph

  1. Introduce it with a signal phrase. Name the author and say why they matter.
  2. Quote, paraphrase, or summarize, and add an MLA in-text citation.
  3. Explain what the source shows in your own words.
  4. Connect it to a specific case detail. Say whether it confirms, complicates, or contradicts that detail.
  5. Tie it to your thesis.
Model (invented topic and invented source, for practice only)

Food marketing researcher Lena Ortiz, who studies meal kit companies, found that most customers cancel within six months (Ortiz 14). That finding complicates the FreshFork packet's promise that its boxes "build lifelong cooking habits" (slide 5). If most people quit that fast, the habit story works more as branding than as evidence. This gap supports my thesis that FreshFork sells a feeling of belonging more than a real change in how people cook.

Search tips (short)

  • Use AND to combine ideas, OR for words that mean the same thing, and quotation marks to keep a phrase together.
  • Turn on a peer reviewed or scholarly journals filter when the database has one.
  • Read the abstract first. Check the date and the author.
  • Google Scholar tip: open the menu, go to Settings, then Library links. Search for San Antonio College, check "San Antonio College Library - Read at SAC Library!" and click Save. Off campus, you sign in with your ACES login.
