Student prompts: essay overview and practice sequence

How the Case Study / critical analysis sequence works. Use these checkpoints to practice; your instructor will confirm what to submit and when in class.

Essay overview. Choose one case study packet — not only a topic label — and stay with it. A case study is one real situation with clear edges and evidence. Your packet is the main text.

Critical analysis means judging how the case holds up:

  • Framing — How does the packet define the problem?
  • Stakeholders — Who is affected? Whose voices appear or are missing?
  • Evidence quality — Data vs branding, opinion, or a single example
  • Assumptions — What the case takes for granted
  • Limits — What the packet can and cannot tell us

Your thesis answers one critical question about your packet. Body paragraphs argue by interpreting evidence. Summary tells what the packet says; analysis tells what it means and where it is strong or thin. Aim for about 1,000 to 1,500 words MLA with 1 to 2 outside sources beyond the packet.

Practice sequence (shapes only):

  1. Topic selection + preview — Topic folder and exact packet title; one-sentence summary; critical question; working thesis; two concrete packet details; why you chose this case.
  2. Critical analysis reading notes — Notes under the five lenses; at least two original critical questions; end with “A possible focus for my essay is …” Class models (BarkBox / Crumbl) show the method only.
  3. Outline / research plan — Working thesis; outline with intro, ≥3 body sections (topic sentence + packet details + analysis), limitations / ethics, conclusion; 1 to 2 outside sources and what each adds.
  4. Working draft + one peer review — Continuous prose toward the final length; peer pass restates thesis, names the critical question, strongest evidence, thinner analysis, limitations, and two revision moves.
  5. Final critical analysis essay — Intro + thesis; analysis body; outside sources; limitations / ethics; conclusion; Works Cited. Not a summary-only report, not a classical argument dossier, not a mid-essay topic switch.

Sample critical-question shapes:

  • In [packet], which statements about [X] rest on measurable data, and which rest on branding or a single example?
  • How does [packet] frame the problem of [Y], and what would change if a different stakeholder framed it?
  • Whose voices appear in [packet], and whose are missing — and how does that shape what the case can prove?
  • What does [packet] take for granted about [Z], and where does that assumption leave the evidence thin?

Library search tips

Prefer SAC Library databases when your essay needs outside sources. Start with a topic phrase plus one tension word, then narrow by peer-reviewed or full text. Sign in with ACES when prompted. Ask a librarian if a paywall appears; campus access often unlocks it.

  • Use the keyword generator first, then paste a draft query into the library search or a database (Academic Search Complete, JSTOR, and similar).
  • Try subject headings or Subjects filters once you find one good article.
  • Save citations carefully; verify every MLA entry against the source and Purdue OWL or your handbook.

Google Scholar search help

Scholar is useful for discovering scholarly articles and tracking citations. Pair it with SAC access: open Scholar, then Settings, Library links, and search Alamo or San Antonio College so Full-Text links appear. Prefer library PDFs over random upload sites.

  • Quote exact phrases: "subscription fatigue"
  • Combine concepts with AND / OR: topic AND tension AND "key phrase"
  • Use the year slider and “Cited by” to find newer or foundational work.
  • If Scholar shows only an abstract, search the title in SAC databases or request help.
