Student prompts: essay overview and practice sequence

How the Case Study / critical analysis sequence works. Use these checkpoints to practice. Your instructor will confirm what to submit and when in class.

Essay overview

  • Choose one case study packet, not only a topic label, and stay with it.
  • A case study is one real situation with clear edges and evidence.
  • Your packet is the main text.
  • Critical analysis means judging how the case holds up.

The five-lens rule

  • Start with your own critical analysis question about your case study.
  • You can find example critical questions in your Case Brief file.
  • Use the five lenses to answer your question.
  • In order, they are Framing, Stakeholders, Evidence, Assumptions, and Limits.
  • Your outline has five body sections, one for each lens, in that order.
  • Put 2 cited case study details in each section.
  • Your thesis answers your question and previews all five lenses, in order.
  • Your case study packet is your primary source. Use it in every body section.
  • Use exactly 2 outside sources from Google Scholar or the SAC Library.
  • Every body section uses your packet plus one or both outside sources.
  • You can use either outside source in any section, and as often as you need.
  • Put your limitations or ethics idea in the Limits section.
  • Do not add a separate limitations section outside of Limits.
  • The Outline / Research Plan assignment in Canvas is the graded turn-in.

The five lenses, in this order, are:

  • Framing. How does the packet define the problem?
  • Stakeholders. Who is affected? Whose voices appear, and whose are missing?
  • Evidence. Is the evidence data, or is it branding, opinion, or a single example?
  • Assumptions. What does the case take for granted?
  • Limits. What the packet can and cannot tell us, including gaps, biases, limitations, and ethical tensions. There is not a separate limitations or ethics section.

Analysis, not summary

  • Body sections argue by interpreting evidence.
  • Summary tells what the packet says.
  • Analysis tells what it means and where it is strong or thin.

Length and sources

  • Aim for about 1,000 to 1,500 words MLA.
  • Use exactly 2 outside sources beyond the packet. Every body section uses your packet plus one or both of them.

Practice sequence (shapes only):

  1. Topic selection + preview. Topic folder and exact packet title, one-sentence summary, and a critical question you wrote yourself. Do not lock a thesis yet.
  2. Critical analysis reading notes. Notes under all five lenses, at least two original critical questions, and an ending line: “A possible focus for my essay is …” Class models (BarkBox / Crumbl) show the method only.
  3. Outline / research plan. Working thesis that previews all five lenses, in order. Outline with intro, five body sections in that order, and conclusion. Each lens section has two cited packet details. Every body section uses the packet plus one or both of your exactly 2 outside sources. You can use either source in any section, and as often as you need. This is the graded turn-in in Canvas.
  4. Working draft + one peer review. Continuous prose toward the final length. Peer pass restates thesis, names the critical question, strongest evidence, thinner analysis, limits, and two revision moves.
  5. Final critical analysis essay. Intro and thesis that preview all five lenses, in order, five body sections in that order, exactly 2 outside sources used across the five body sections, conclusion, and Works Cited. Not a summary-only report, not a classical argument dossier, and not a mid-essay topic switch.

Sample critical-question shapes:

  • In [packet], which statements about [X] rest on measurable data, and which rest on branding or a single example?
  • How does [packet] frame the problem of [Y], and what would change if a different stakeholder framed it?
  • Whose voices appear in [packet], and whose are missing, and how does that shape what the case can prove?
  • What does [packet] take for granted about [Z], and where does that assumption leave the evidence thin?

Library search tips

Prefer SAC Library databases when your essay needs outside sources. Start with a topic phrase plus one tension word, then narrow by peer-reviewed or full text. Sign in with ACES when prompted. Ask a librarian if a paywall appears; campus access often unlocks it.

  • Use the keyword generator first, then paste a draft query into the library search or a database (Academic Search Complete, JSTOR, and similar).
  • Try subject headings or Subjects filters once you find one good article.
  • Save citations carefully; verify every MLA entry against the source and Purdue OWL or your handbook.

Google Scholar search help

Scholar is useful for discovering scholarly articles and tracking citations. Pair it with SAC access: open Scholar, then Settings, Library links, and search Alamo or San Antonio College so Full-Text links appear. Prefer library PDFs over random upload sites.

  • Quote exact phrases: "subscription fatigue"
  • Combine concepts with AND / OR: topic AND tension AND "key phrase"
  • Use the year slider and “Cited by” to find newer or foundational work.
  • If Scholar shows only an abstract, search the title in SAC databases or request help.
